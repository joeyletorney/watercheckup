## 1. Read a water report without turning a detection into a scare

**2–3 minutes.** Open a current public supplier report, show its name and reporting year, then one analyte row. Say what the units, result, sampling period, reporting threshold and comparison value mean. Distinguish a detected substance, a benchmark comparison and a formally determined violation. A missing current feed means unverified, not safe or unsafe. Explain why the supplier report does not measure lead introduced by one home's plumbing. End by showing where to find the current supplier report and when a specific tap test would answer the remaining question.

**Needed before publishing:** chosen real source URL/report, exact row and dates, Joe's recording. Avoid showing addresses, account information or private lab identifiers. Include source and date on screen. Do not invent numbers or reuse a historical sample as a current reading.

**Useful post draft:** “A detection in a water report is the start of a question. Here is how I read the date, units and comparison value before deciding what it means.” Link to the relevant report interpretation once recorded. Identify ownership of WaterCheckup when sharing.

## 2. A TDS reading is not a lead or PFAS test

**60–90 seconds.** Show a TDS meter only if one is available. Explain that it measures electrical conductivity converted to an estimate of dissolved solids; it does not identify individual contaminants. If filming samples, label source, time and instrument. Present actual readings only. Do not claim a lower reading proves safer water, lead removal or PFAS reduction. Show how to check an exact cartridge's published reduction claim instead.

**Needed:** meter, clean sample vessels, real readings if demonstrated, link to the model-specific performance sheet. If no equipment, record a document walkthrough and label it an explanation, not a test.

**Useful post draft:** “A TDS meter can help track dissolved solids. It cannot tell you whether lead or PFAS is present. Here is what I check instead before choosing a filter.”

## 3. Before buying a filter: fit and recurring cost

**2–3 minutes.** Show the actual space and equipment only if available, or use the manufacturer's dimension drawing with clear attribution. Walk through counter clearance, outlet, refilling and reject water for countertop RO; cabinet, faucet and drain needs for under-sink RO; main-line access and regeneration for softeners. Use the guide's explicit one-gallon/day illustration to explain replacement costs. State the price-check date and separate annualized filter allowance from first-year spending. Do not pretend to have installed or tested equipment you do not own.

**Needed:** real installation space or official drawing, exact model/manual, dated cartridge prices. The three revised guides provide linked evidence and cost assumptions for reference.

**Useful post draft:** “The purchase price is only one part of a filter decision. Check whether it fits, how you will refill or install it, and which replacement filters you will actually need.”
